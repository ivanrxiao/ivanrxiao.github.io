I study urban freight and its effects on city streets, land use, and the electric grid. My research covers curb management and urban deliveries, warehouse zoning and industrial land use, and charging infrastructure for electric trucks.

I build quantitative models with vehicle routing optimization, traffic microsimulation, spatial econometrics, machine learning, and language-model text extraction. The data come from vehicle GPS trajectories, curb inventories, zoning codes, and property records.

At USC I work with Genevieve Giuliano and Marlon Boarnet. I received my Ph.D. in Civil and Environmental Engineering from the University of California, Davis in 2025, advised by Miguel Jaller.

Research

My research covers freight activity at three connected scales. Each scale sits inside the next. A curb sits within a city, and a city sits within a regional freight and power network.

Street and curb

Delivery trucks, passenger cars, and ride-hailing vehicles compete for limited curb space in dense districts. At USC I am building a curb digital twin of downtown Los Angeles with traffic microsimulation (SUMO). The model simulates 209 delivery stops from business registry, employment, and curb inventory data.

Even with enough total loading capacity for all deliveries, mismatches across blocks and hours of the day still cause widespread double parking. Loading zone reallocation, shared off-street docks, and off-peak deliveries reduce illegal parking.

Related: parking search cruising, delivery and shopping travel emissions to 2050

City and corridor

Warehouse growth in Southern California has led many cities to tighten industrial zoning. Zoning codes are legal texts and vary widely across cities, so regional comparison is difficult.

I used large language model (LLM) text extraction, with manual verification of the ordinances, to code industrial zoning rules in about 150 jurisdictions across four counties. The coded rules are linked to warehouse sales and leases. Multilevel hedonic models then estimate the relationship between zoning rules and industrial property values.

Related: warehouse zoning project, spatial analysis of logistics performance

Region and grid

The shift from diesel to electric trucks depends on charging locations and on grid capacity. I built an electric truck routing model (ALNS-eVRP) informed by more than 4.3 million California truckload records.

With low-power depot charging alone, empty (deadhead) mileage rises by 10.5% at high electric truck adoption relative to diesel. Public fast charging at 350 kW lowers deadhead miles by about 5% and charging stops by about 14%. Current work adds plug limits, time-of-use rates, and grid capacity to the routing model.

Related: truck charging infrastructure, truck parking and idling in environmental justice communities, policy brief
